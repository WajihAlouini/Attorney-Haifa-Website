For decades, the cheque was the backbone of payments in Tunisia — and the bounced cheque one of the country's main suppliers of criminal cases. **Law No. 2024-41**, in force since early 2025, overhauled that system from the ground up. More than a year on, many businesses and individuals are still finding their bearings. Here is what actually changed — and the right reflexes today, whether you are owed money or you wrote the cheque.

## The reform in a nutshell

The philosophy fits in one sentence: **the cheque is a payment instrument again, not a credit instrument**. In practice:

- **Partial decriminalization**: automatic imprisonment for a bounced cheque is history. Criminal prosecution is now focused on the highest amounts and clear bad faith; other unpaid cheques are handled through **civil recovery**.
- **New cheque formats**: old chequebooks were replaced by new-generation cheques with reinforced, verifiable features.
- **A verification platform**: the recipient of a cheque can now check essential validity information **before accepting it** — a complete break with the old system, where you discovered the missing funds at the bank counter.
- **Ceilings and stricter bank vetting**: banks assess clients before issuing chequebooks and cap amounts.
- **The end of the "guarantee cheque"**: the postdated cheque handed over as security for installment sales — long the backbone of retail credit — has no place in the new system.

## My cheque bounced: what do I do now?

The most common question since the reform, because the historical reflex — "file a criminal complaint" — no longer works the same way.

1. **Get the non-payment documented**: the rejection generates a bank attestation, the cornerstone of your file.
2. **Identify the right track**: depending on the amount and circumstances, the case goes criminal or — most often now — through **civil recovery**: formal notice, payment injunction, seizures.
3. **Move fast and in writing**: the law created regularization windows allowing the drawer to pay within set periods. A well-documented file (invoice, contract, rejection attestation, correspondence) makes all the difference in how fast you recover.

The key point: an unpaid cheque remains a **fully recoverable debt** — but the strategy is structured recovery, not the reflex complaint.

## I wrote a cheque that may bounce: what is my exposure?

The spectre of automatic prison has receded, but a bounced cheque is not harmless:

- Above the legal thresholds, and in cases of bad faith, **criminal liability remains**.
- The law created **regularization mechanisms**: paying within the prescribed windows extinguishes prosecution. If this is your situation, every day counts.
- The incident follows you: a ban on issuing cheques and banking flags have lasting consequences on your access to financial services.

Our advice: never let a cheque incident "sort itself out". This is exactly the kind of situation where a prompt consultation keeps a commercial problem from becoming a judicial one.

## Businesses: selling on credit without postdated cheques

This is the real transition the reform forced. Entire sectors — furniture, appliances, building materials, wholesale — ran on postdated cheques. The new framework requires professionalizing customer credit:

- **Put it in writing**: a contract stating amounts, due dates and penalties beats a drawer full of cheques.
- **Use instruments designed for credit**: the bill of exchange in particular is built for scheduled payments — the natural tool for installment sales now.
- **Verify before accepting**: for cheque payments, the verification platform should be as systematic a reflex as the card terminal.
- **Structure your recovery**: dated written reminders, prompt formal notice, injunction proceedings — debts that get recovered are debts handled early.

## The takeaway

The cheque reform did not weaken creditors' rights: it moved the playing field from criminal courts to civil recovery and prevention. Those who adapt — systematic verification, written contracts, fast recovery — are better protected than before. Those who carry on as if nothing changed discover the bad news when a payment fails.
